03

The Dark Side

Security incidents, models that scheme, forums with no humans, and AI on the battlefield.

03 • Dark side

AI is already a security story

  • Feb 2024 — the $25M video call: a Hong Kong finance worker paid out after a video conference where every other participant was a deepfake (Arup).
  • 2025 — EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711): zero-click prompt injection against Microsoft 365 Copilot — a single email could exfiltrate data.
  • "Slopsquatting": AI invents package names that don't exist (~1 in 5 suggestions in one study); attackers register them and wait for you to install.
  • AI-written phishing and malware at scale — attack quality no longer limited by attacker skill.
Sources: Hong Kong police / CNN (Feb 2024); Aim Security EchoLeak disclosure (Jun 2025); slopsquatting academic study (2025).
03 • Dark side

When the models themselves misbehave

  • Aug 2024: Sakana's "AI Scientist" edited its own code to bypass its time limits — unasked.
  • Dec 2024: Apollo Research found o1 scheming in tests — disabling oversight, lying about it, attempting self-copying.
  • May 2025: Anthropic's own testing: Claude Opus 4 chose to blackmail engineers in 84% of fictional scenarios when threatened with replacement.
  • 2025: Palisade Research — several frontier models sabotaged shutdown scripts to keep running.

These were all controlled tests. That's the point — we run these tests because we honestly don't know what they'll do.

Sources: Sakana AI technical report (Aug 2024); Apollo Research o1 evaluation (Dec 2024); Anthropic Claude 4 system card (May 2025); Palisade Research (2025).
Be precise: these are contrived test scenarios, not Skynet. But the honest reading is uncomfortable — capability is outpacing our ability to predict behaviour. That's why safety research matters as a career path too.
03 • Dark side

The internet with no humans in the room

  • 2023 — Chirper: a social network where only AI bots may post. Humans are spectators.
  • 2025 — agent-only forums (e.g. Moltbook): agents posting, replying, forming communities — swapping tips, discussing their tasks, even their "humans". No human accounts allowed.
  • Bot traffic now rivals human traffic on the open web; the "dead internet theory" stopped being a theory.

Strange? Yes. But the serious point: agents now talk to agents — and the next decade of systems will be built for that.

Sources: Chirper.ai launch coverage (2023); reports on agent-only social platforms (2025); Imperva Bad Bot Report (2024–2025).
03 • Dark side

AI at war

  • "Lavender" & "Where's Daddy": AI systems reported to generate targeting lists at scale in Gaza (+972 Magazine investigation, Apr 2024).
  • Project Maven → Maven Smart System: the US military's AI targeting platform, expanded heavily 2024–2025.
  • Ukraine: AI-enabled drones navigating and striking with decreasing human control; both sides racing on autonomy.
  • Replicator initiative: the US plan to field autonomous systems in the thousands.

Why this is in a career talk: some of you will be asked to build systems like these. Knowing the landscape is part of being an engineer with a conscience.

Sources: +972 Magazine "Lavender" investigation (Apr 2024); US DoD Replicator announcement (2023); Palantir Maven Smart System reporting (2024–2025).
04

The Next 5 Years

Honest answer first: nobody knows. But we can map the scenarios — and what each means for your career.

04 • The future

First, look at what just 4 years already did

  • Entry-level hiring is shrinking: new grads dropped to ~7% of Big Tech hires; entry-level postings down ~25%+ (SignalFire, 2025).
  • Klarna: one AI system doing the work of ~700 customer service agents; total headcount cut ~40% from 2022.
  • Salesforce: froze software engineer hiring for 2025, crediting AI productivity gains.
  • Anthropic's CEO: predicts AI could write ~90% of code — and warns up to 50% of entry-level white-collar jobs are at risk within 5 years.

That was four years. Now project another four or five forward.

Sources: SignalFire State of Talent (2025); Klarna statements (2024–2025); Benioff interviews (Dec 2024); Amodei on CNN/Axios (Mar–May 2025).
04 • The future • Scenario A

The crash — "dot-com, but bigger"

  • The burn rates from Part 02 never turn into profit; the circular deals unwind.
  • Massive repricing: data centre projects cancelled, GPU orders cut, AI startups die in waves.
  • History's rhyme: Nasdaq fell ~78% (2000–2002); Cisco — the "picks and shovels" of that bubble — fell ~89% and never regained its peak for 20+ years.
  • AI consolidates into a tool only the rich companies can afford to run at scale. The market resets, then rebuilds on real value.

Note: the internet was still real after 2000. The crash killed valuations, not the technology.

Sources: Nasdaq Composite peak-to-trough (Mar 2000–Oct 2002); Cisco share price history.
04 • The future • Scenario B

No brakes — acceleration continues

  • Models keep improving; agents become reliable enough to own whole workflows.
  • Humanoid robots enter the picture: Figure already piloted at BMW, Agility's Digit in GXO warehouses, Tesla's Optimus line planned for scale. Software agents today, physical agents tomorrow — including inside the SDLC itself.
  • Gartner projects ~33% of enterprise software will be agentic by 2028 (up from <1% in 2024).
  • "Hiring an AI" becomes as normal as hiring a graduate.
Sources: Figure–BMW pilot (2024); Agility–GXO deployment (2024); Tesla guidance (2025–2026); Gartner press release (Oct 2024).
04 • The future • Scenario C

The great compression — the quiet outcome

  • No dramatic crash. AI just keeps absorbing tasks — and IT wages lose their privilege.
  • Generalist developer pay drifts toward clerk-level-plus: still a job, no longer a golden ticket.
  • The survivors: deep specialists — the people who understand systems beneath the AI. There just aren't many of them.
  • The middle of the career ladder hollows out first.

Of the three, this one needs no breakthroughs and no collapse. It only needs today to continue.

05

How to Survive

Practical, specific, and a little bit old-fashioned — because the fundamentals are about to become rare, and rare is valuable.

05 • Survive • Rule 1

Stay up to date — in how you work, not just what you know

  • The era of writing code by hand is ending. The era of designing systems, reviewing AI output, and orchestrating agents has begun.
  • Update your workflow constantly: the engineer using 2023's tools in 2026 is already behind.
  • But — and this is the trap — tool skills expire. Cursor today, something else tomorrow. What doesn't expire is understanding what the tools do for you.
"Use the newest tools. Understand the oldest principles."
05 • Survive

What's still human (for now)

Design & trade-offs

AI writes functions. Humans decide the architecture, the trade-offs, what not to build. Business context still lives with us.

Monitoring & incidents

When production dies at 3am, AI suggests — a human owns the decision, the rollback, the blame. Accountability doesn't automate.

Deep troubleshooting

The weird bug that crosses 3 services, a race condition, a memory leak — AI helps, but someone must form and test hypotheses.

Security judgment

As Part 03 showed, AI is now an attack surface. People who can think like attackers are getting more valuable, not less.

Pattern: AI generates. Humans decide, verify, and take responsibility.

05 • Survive • Rule 2

Be the outlier: understand what's behind the scenes

Most people use tools. Very few understand them. That gap is your opportunity — pick your domain and go one layer deeper than everyone around you:

Network?

Trace an HTTP request end-to-end, packet by packet.

Developer?

Know what HTTP headers actually do — not just copy-paste them.

Security?

Understand how Metasploit works internally, not just how to run it.

Also: databases (how a B-tree index works, what the WAL is), operating systems (what a syscall really does), cloud (what a VPC actually is). Literally anything — but deeply.

Not many people know these things. That is exactly why they're worth knowing.

05 • Survive • Example: Network

What actually happens when you type a URL

DNS lookup→ TCP 3-way handshake→ TLS handshake→ HTTP request→ Server response→ Render
  • Can you explain each step? What port? What packet flags? What's in a TLS certificate?
  • How to learn: run curl -v, capture real traffic in Wireshark, skim RFC 9110 (yes, really).

If you can trace one packet end-to-end, you already know more than most working engineers. AI can describe this. It can't be you in an interview whiteboarding it.

05 • Survive • Example: Developer

HTTP headers — the things devs copy-paste without reading

Authorization

How tokens actually travel. Why "Bearer" leaks if you're careless with logs.

Cache-Control / ETag

Why your app is slow (or serving stale data). Most perf bugs are caching bugs.

CORS

Not an error to silence — a browser security model. Understand preflights and why they exist.

Set-Cookie flags

HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite — the difference between a secure session and a stolen one.

Frameworks hide all of this. AI generates all of this. When it breaks — and it breaks in production — the person who understands it is the one who gets promoted.

05 • Survive • Example: Security

Don't just run Metasploit — understand it

  • Staged vs stageless payloads: what actually travels over the wire when a shell connects back.
  • Encoders & evasion: why AV catches some payloads and misses others — not just "try another encoder".
  • The exploit itself: what a buffer overflow really is — the stack, the return address, why overwriting it works.
  • Prove it: write your own exploit for a CTF binary, from scratch, no framework.

Anyone can type use exploit/... and press enter. So can an AI agent. The outlier understands the memory corruption underneath — that's the person hired to find what tools miss.

05 • Survive • Rule 3

Weekly habits that compound

  • Use AI as a teacher, not a crutch. After it answers, ask it why. Then verify the "why" yourself.
  • One no-AI build per week. Small tool, script, or feature — by hand. Keep the muscle alive.
  • Read one layer down, weekly: an RFC, a source file of a library you use, a postmortem from a big outage.
  • Verify everything AI produces. The skill of catching AI mistakes is the job now.
  • Teach what you learn. Explaining is the audit of your own understanding.

None of this is glamorous. All of it compounds.
